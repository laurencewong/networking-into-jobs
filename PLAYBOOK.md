# The playbook: situation by situation

The guide above is the why. This is the how. It takes the same method and walks through specific situations you'll actually hit while networking into a job: an ex-colleague at the target company, a recruiter who went quiet, the call itself, the referral ask, what to do when someone says no.

This is a synthesis of free, public teaching from Steve Dalton, Austin Belcak, Madeline Mann, Phyl Terry, J.T. O'Donnell, Jeff Su, Lenny Rachitsky, and the Harvard/Stanford/Berkeley career centers. See `README.md` for the full method and `SOURCES.md` for citations. Nothing here is independently verified statistics. Where a source cites a number (Belcak's 80%/40% referral-hire estimates, Su's 84% weak-ties figure), treat it as that source's own claim, not ground truth.

Every example message below is a fictional illustration for a generic senior product manager. Replace every bracket with your own real detail before you send anything, and never send a bracket.

**The one rule that makes everything else possible: you send every message yourself.** An assistant can draft, track, and remind. It cannot send on your behalf. Nothing in this playbook authorizes automated or mass outreach.

## Rules

1. **No lying.** One invented claim costs the referral and the relationship the moment anyone checks it, and in rooms this small, someone eventually checks.
2. **You send every message yourself. No automation, no mass outreach, no bot-sent connection requests.** The entire mechanism only works because a real person chose to write to this person, not because a script did.
3. **Perspective first. Never a referral ask in message 1.** Every career-services source this method draws from (Harvard, Stanford, Berkeley, Dalton, Belcak, O'Donnell) treats an early job ask as close to disqualifying.
4. **Every first message carries one specific detail about them, one real point in common, one offer, and one easy either-or question.** This is where Belcak, Mann, and Su's independently built playbooks converge. It's the shape that earns a one-line reply instead of silence.
5. **Under 90 words. Connection notes under 300 characters.** Length alone signals pitch versus question. A note this short is one a busy person can actually answer from their phone.
6. **Comment on their public work first when there's something real to comment on.** Mann's rule: be a familiar name before the connection request shows up, not a stranger asking for something.
7. **Follow up on day 3 and day 7 (Dalton's 3B7 Routine), then stop.** Reply odds drop sharply after day 3. Day 7 is a free, low-cost second touch. A third message reads as pressure, not persistence.
8. **Cap new outreach at 5 first messages a week.** Sized for someone working full time. Keeps 5 to 10 live conversations running at once instead of 50 shallow, forgettable ones. See cadence and metrics below for why 5.
9. **Discretion while you're still employed.** The search only works if it never costs you the job you're searching from. No internal names, no unannounced plans, no numbers or details your employer hasn't made public.
10. **Always give before asking.** Belcak's "value equation": a comment, a real offer, a piece of feedback, before any ask. It's reciprocity, and it's what earns the next ask.
11. **Log every contact and every promise in a tracker.** Nobody can hold whose turn it is to follow up in their head across a dozen live threads.
12. **Never ask the same person for the same thing twice.** Repeating a declined ask reads as not having listened the first time, and it's the fastest way to lose a contact for good.
13. **Match the size of the ask to the size of the relationship.** Berkeley's framing: ask for directions, not a ride. A cold note earns a question. A referral is earned by an actual conversation.
14. **Keep anything confidential or unannounced out of every message.** No internal figures, no comp history, no colleague names, nothing your employer treats as not for external use. A networking message is still an external message.
15. **Vary the specific detail in every message. No copy-paste verbatim.** Mann and O'Donnell both flag this: recipients recognize a form letter instantly and stop reading.

## Situations

Each situation below: when it applies, what to do, what to avoid, and a worked example. Every quoted message here is a fictional illustration, written for a generic senior product manager persona. Fill in your own real details; don't send a message with brackets still in it.

### 1. An ex-colleague now at the target company

**When it applies:** someone you actually worked with at a past employer, now at a company on your list.
**What to do:** name the shared employer and years honestly, state the new target role in one line, ask one question only they'd have a useful angle on.
**What to avoid:** implying you worked together directly if your dates didn't actually overlap. Check that before sending.

> Hi [Name], I saw you went from product at [Former Company] to [Target Company]. I was at [Former Company] from [year] to [year], working on loyalty and personalization. These days I lead a team building AI-driven product recommendations. A [role] opening at [Target Company] caught my eye and I'd like your read first. No referral ask. Does product work there feel closer to [Former Company]-style growth, or to a platform team?

Connection note: *"Hi [Name], fellow [Former Company] alum. I was there [year] to [year] on loyalty and personalization. Now leading an AI recommendations team. Curious how the move to [Target Company] went for you. Not a referral ask."*

### 2. An alumni cold contact

**When it applies:** no shared employer, but a confirmed school connection, your one formal credential-based "weak tie," per Su's alumni-filter tactic.
**What to do:** lead with the real hook (their post, interview, or work), mention your shared school as the second, smaller point of connection, not the headline.
**What to avoid:** leading with "fellow alum" before you've shown you actually know their work. It reads as using the school as the whole pitch.

> Hi [Name], I read your [interview/post] on [specific topic]. Fellow [School] grad here. I lead a team building AI-driven product recommendations, and I'm weighing platform-side AI roles against company-side ones. Looking for perspective, not a referral. You've seen it from inside and now from outside. Which teaches you more about customers? The platform with every query, or the company with the full purchase history?

Connection note: *"Hi [Name], fellow [School] grad. Read your [interview/post] on [topic]. I lead a team building AI recommendations, weighing platform vs. company-side roles. Would value your perspective sometime. Not asking for a referral."*

### 3. The hiring manager who posted the role publicly

**When it applies:** someone posted "I'm hiring for X" themselves, the lowest-friction door there is, since they've already signaled they want applicants to reach out.
**What to do:** reference the specific thing that connects your background to their stated need; ask a real question about the problem space, not "is the role still open."
**What to avoid:** asking for the job directly just because they posted it publicly. The posting doesn't cancel rule 3.

> Hi [Name], I saw [their post] on [Target Company] using AI to help customers build and reorder carts. I built a similar feature at [Former Company] from [year] to [year], and now lead a team on product recommendations. I'd value your view on where this goes. Not asking for a referral. Does this win first on repeat purchases, or on considered ones? Happy to share a short write-up from that side of it.

Connection note: *"Hi [Name], I built a cart and reorder feature at [Former Company] ([year] to [year]) and now lead a product recommendations team. Following [their post]'s work closely. Would value your perspective sometime. Not asking for a referral."*

### 4. A team member who posts on social media

**When it applies:** not the hiring manager, but a peer-level IC or staff-level person on the target team who's publicly active and ideally has explicitly invited messages on a relevant topic.
**What to do:** take them up on their own invitation if they made one; keep the content specific to what they actually posted.
**What to avoid:** pitching yourself for the role to a peer with no hiring authority. The ask here is perspective, nothing more.

> Hi [Name], your profile says to message you if you run your work with AI agents, so I'm taking you up on it. I run a handful of agents for my own work, and none of them can spend money without my approval. My day job is leading a product recommendations team. Perspective only, no referral ask. Is someone like me more useful to you as a test case, or as a design critic?

Connection note: *"Hi [Name], you said to message you if I run my work with agents. I run a handful, and none can spend money without my approval. Happy to explain why. I lead a product recommendations team. Perspective only, no referral ask."*

### 5. A recruiter who reached out to you

**When it applies:** inbound from a recruiter, cold or forwarded.
**What to do:** respond quickly, answer with real background in one line, ask one clarifying either-or question about the role so your next message isn't a guess, offer something concrete if you have it (a write-up, a relevant project).
**What to avoid:** going silent on inbound interest. Recruiters move to the next candidate fast, and this is the one situation where speed actually matters more than polish.

> Hi [Name], thanks for reaching out. I lead a team building AI-driven product recommendations, and before that ran loyalty and promotions at [Former Company]. Happy to take a look. Is the role closer to growth and incentives, or AI and recommendations? I have a short write-up on this kind of work if it's useful context.

### 6. Reaching a recruiter cold (Su's sequence)

**When it applies:** no warm path at all into a target company, the last resort, not the first move.
**What to do:** Su's two-step. Connect first without mentioning the role at all. Only after they accept, ask to be connected to someone actually doing the job for a casual chat.
**What to avoid:** mentioning the specific role in the first touch. That's what makes this different from, and weaker than, situation 5.

Connection note: *"Hi [Name], I noticed you recruit for the team hiring for [function] at [Company]. I lead a team building AI-driven product recommendations and we're both [shared detail, e.g. based in the same city]. Would like to connect."*

Follow-up DM after they accept: *"Thanks for connecting. I've been reading about the team's work and wanted to understand the day to day before going further. Would you be able to connect me with someone actually on the team for a short, casual chat?"*

### 7. No reply after 2 follow-ups

**When it applies:** day 3 and day 7 have both passed with silence. This is the situation most people never reach because they stop after message 1.
**What to do:** send exactly two follow-ups on the 3B7 cadence, then stop and move to the next person at the same target company. Log "no response x2" in the tracker so nobody re-sends by accident.
**What to avoid:** a third message. Berkeley and Harvard both land on two attempts as the ceiling; past that it's pressure, not persistence.

Day 3: *"Hi [Name], following up in case this got buried. No worries if now isn't a good time. Happy to send one specific question instead of asking for a call."*

Day 7: *"Hi [Name], one more try, no pressure either way. If you ever have ten minutes down the road I'd still value your view on [topic]. Hope things are going well at [Company]."*

Then stop. That means moving to another contact at the same company, not re-sending the same person a third time.

### 8. They replied "happy to chat"

**When it applies:** the reply you want. Now the job is logistics, not persuasion.
**What to do:** lock a specific length (20 to 30 minutes) and let them pick the time; say what you'll bring so the call doesn't feel open-ended to them.
**What to avoid:** proposing an hour, or being vague about what you want to cover. Both raise the size of the ask right when you should be lowering it.

> That's great, thank you [Name]. Would 20 minutes next Tuesday or Wednesday work better for you? I'll bring a short list of questions on [topic] and keep us to the time.

### 9. After the call (thank-you within 24 hours)

**When it applies:** every real conversation, no exceptions, inside 24 to 48 hours.
**What to do:** name one specific thing you took from the call, not a generic thanks; deliver on anything you offered in the room in the same message if you can.
**What to avoid:** a thank-you so generic it reads as a task you're checking off. Mann's note: specificity is what makes someone remember you later.

> Hi [Name], thank you for the time today. Your point about [specific thing they said] is staying with me. I'm attaching the [write-up/notes] I mentioned. I'll keep you posted on where this goes.

### 10. The referral ask after a good call

**When it applies:** an established relationship, at least one real conversation, ideally more, and a real open role you've actually applied to or are about to.
**What to do:** ask directly, acknowledge what it costs them to vouch for you, and explicitly release the pressure.
**What to avoid:** asking cold, in message 1, or before you've had the conversation. This is the one ask in this whole playbook that needs the relationship built first.

> Hi [Name], I wanted to ask you directly. Would you be comfortable referring me for the [role] at [Company]? I know referring someone reflects on you, so no pressure if it doesn't feel right. Happy to just apply on my own either way.

### 11. They say "no openings right now"

**When it applies:** a real "no," not a brush-off. The role doesn't exist, the headcount's frozen, whatever the reason.
**What to do:** downgrade the ask instead of pushing, per O'Donnell and Berkeley. Ask for advice on strengthening a future application instead of a referral to a role that isn't there.
**What to avoid:** treating "no openings" as a door closed on the relationship. It's a door closed on one specific ask.

> Totally understand, and thanks for being straight with me. If it's easier, do you have any advice on how I could strengthen a future application to the [team] team, or know a better way in?

### 12. They offer a referral before you ask

**When it applies:** rare, and the best possible outcome. Someone volunteers it unprompted, usually because you've already given them something or the conversation went well on its own.
**What to do:** accept gracefully, remove friction (don't make them chase you for anything), and use the moment to ask the closing "who else" question while you have their goodwill.
**What to avoid:** over-explaining why you'd be a good fit right after they've already said yes. Let the offer stand on its own.

> That would mean a lot, thank you. Send it whenever works for you, no rush. One more thing while I have you. Is there anyone else at [Company] you think I should talk to about [topic]?

### 13. A warm intro request through a mutual

**When it applies:** a real, specific, findable connection between someone you already know and the target company. For example, your former employer shipped a real, named partnership or integration with the target company, and someone from your old network may have touched it directly. This beats any cold note into the target company, but you have to do this lookup yourself; nobody else can see your own network for you.
**What to do:** name the specific, verifiable thing that connects them to the target (not a guess), ask for the intro directly but release it if it's not theirs to make.
**What to avoid:** implying the target company's product uses "your" work just because you worked adjacent to it. State only what's true.

> Hi [Name], quick one. I saw [Former Company] shipped [a specific, verifiable partnership or feature] with [Target Company] and wondered if you touched that. I'm looking at a product role at [Target Company] and would value a warm intro if you know anyone there. No pressure if not. Happy to swap notes on where that work has landed since I left.

### 14. Re-engaging a dormant contact

**When it applies:** someone you had one good conversation with weeks or months ago and let the thread go quiet. The most common failure mode in the whole process, per the source guide, and the easiest one to fix.
**What to do:** report back on whether you acted on their advice and what happened. This is Mann's and Su's "circling back" move, it reopens the door a flat thank-you note closes.
**What to avoid:** reaching out with no update at all ("just checking in"). That's a weaker version of the same move and reads as having nothing new to say.

> Hi [Name], it's been a while. I took your advice from our call this spring to focus my search on AI-forward companies instead of pure retail, and it's where my best conversations have come from since. Wanted to close the loop and say thanks.

### 15. A senior or executive target

**When it applies:** a VP or senior director who's realistically a skip-level or higher relative to any role you'd land.
**What to do:** comment on their public work instead of cold-messaging. At this seniority a thoughtful comment is the appropriate-sized move; a cold DM asking for their time is not.
**What to avoid:** a direct ask of any kind in a first touch. The size-of-ask-matches-size-of-relationship rule (13) applies hardest here.

Comment on a post, not a DM: *"Customers increasingly start from a photo instead of a text query, and in categories where fit and detail matter, that makes it even more true. Curious how much of the roadmap here is chasing that shift versus feeding it."*

### 16. When someone asks what you're working on while you're still employed

**When it applies:** on a call, in a thread, anywhere, someone asks the obvious follow-up question about your current role.
**What to do:** answer at the level of generality your employer's own public materials allow. Name the broad area, not the specifics. Redirect to the generic, sharable version of the hard problem.
**What to avoid:** naming any unannounced feature, any internal number, or any colleague by name, even to someone you like and trust on the call.

> I lead product for an AI-driven recommendations team, including the roadmap, the evals, and the systems behind it. I can't go into specifics while I'm still there. Happy to talk about what's hard in general, like where evals break down or how grounding holds up under real customer questions.

### 17. Keeping relationships alive after you land a job

**When it applies:** an offer's signed, the search is over, but the network you built isn't disposable.
**What to do:** close the loop with everyone live in the tracker, including people beyond the one who made the actual referral. Name what they specifically gave you and offer to reciprocate.
**What to avoid:** going silent on everyone except the one contact who mattered most. The whole network compounds for next time only if you tend it now.

> Hi [Name], wanted to share I accepted an offer and start next month. Thank you for the time and the conversation a few months back, it shaped how I thought about the move. Happy to return the favor whenever it's useful on your end.

## The call: 20 to 30 minutes

**Opening** (state the time, give context fast, start easy, O'Donnell's low-stakes opener):

> Thanks for making time. I'll keep us to the 20 minutes I asked for. Quick background on me. I lead a team building AI-driven product recommendations, and before that spent about five years running loyalty, promotions, and cart features at a regional retailer. I'm looking at what's next, probably something AI-forward in commerce. Easy one to start. What got you excited about this work in the first place?

**8 sharp questions** (Su's three buckets: the job itself, their path, the industry, plus a sharper version of the generic questions):

1. How is your performance actually measured? A usage number, a revenue number, or something softer?
2. What does the eval process actually look like day to day? Automated, human review, or both?
3. When a feature doesn't perform, who decides whether to kill it or keep iterating?
4. What's the hardest unsolved problem on your roadmap right now, the one nobody's cracked yet?
5. How did you end up in this specific role? What's one turn in your path you didn't see coming?
6. What does the industry outside your company get wrong about this work?
7. Is the harder problem right now getting the model right, or getting the organization to trust what it ships?
8. If you were me, would you weight a platform-side AI role or a company-side one higher right now?

Bring more than 8 if the conversation runs long and wants more. Stanford suggests 5 to 7 for 30 minutes, Harvard says bring up to 15 in reserve. Never bring fewer than you need; a thin question list is the fastest way to turn this into small talk.

**Closing:** thank them by name for something specific they said, confirm anything you owe them (a link, a write-up, a report-back date), and log it before the call leaves your head.

**The line that matters most, every time, even with no specific role in mind:**

> This has been really helpful. Is there anyone else you think I should be talking to, either here or elsewhere in this space?

Su calls skipping this the single biggest miss in the whole process. Harvard and Stanford land on nearly identical phrasing independently, ask it at the end of every real conversation, even the ones that don't feel especially promising.

## Assets you can offer

The rule "always give before asking" (10) only works if there's something real to give. A few things that count, if you actually have them:

| Asset | What it actually is | Offer it to |
|---|---|---|
| An open-source project or code repo | Something real you built, even small, that demonstrates the skill you're selling. A tested project beats a claim on a resume. | Anyone building something adjacent, or a recruiter who asks for proof of hands-on work. |
| A short write-up | A few paragraphs on a real problem you solved or a lesson you learned, specific enough to be useful to someone else sitting in the same seat. | Someone at your target company doing similar work, or someone who asked a question your write-up already answers. |
| A teardown | A close, public read of a product, feature, or company move, done on your own time. | PMs or builders working on something comparable, especially if they posted asking for exactly this kind of outside read. |
| Notes on what you're learning | Whatever you're reading, watching, or studying right now, shared plainly, not polished into a pitch. | Anyone in a more general conversation, or someone who's asked what you read or watch rather than what you build. |
| Anything you've already promised | A write-up, a link, an intro you said you'd send. | The specific person it was promised to, first. Deliver before offering the same kind of thing to someone new. An undelivered promise is worse than no offer at all. |

## Weekly cadence and metrics

**Cadence** (sized for someone employed full time, from the source guide's own recommendation):

- **Monday:** review the target list, add or drop 2 to 3 companies, pick 5 to 10 new contacts from the current targets.
- **Tuesday to Thursday:** send the week's first messages, spread across the days, never all in one sitting.
- **Daily, 10 minutes:** check the tracker for anyone due a day-3 or day-7 follow-up and send those.
- **As calls land:** take them at the length you asked for, send the thank-you inside 24 to 48 hours, log what you learned and what you owe.
- **Friday:** update the tracker, flag who's due a report-back follow-up in 2 to 4 weeks.

**Targets:**

| Metric | Target | Why this number |
|---|---|---|
| New first messages / week | 5, as a cap, not a quota | Matches the cadence above. Keeps 5 to 10 live conversations running, never 50 cold notes. |
| Reply rate, warm (alumni, ex-colleague, inbound recruiter) | 30 to 50% | These paths carry a real point in common. A reply rate well below this means the hook is stale or generic, not that the volume is too low. |
| Reply rate, cold (no connection) | 10 to 20% | Cold outreach is a numbers game by design. Don't read a single cold no-reply as a signal about the message. |
| Calls booked / week | 1 to 3, once the pipeline has warmed up | A realistic senior-level search runs 5 to 10 live conversations at a time, not more. |
| Referrals or warm intros / month | roughly 1 every 3 to 4 weeks | Referrals follow real conversations, not single messages. Don't expect one per call. |

**What to adjust when a number is low:**

- **Reply rate low:** check whether the hook (the post, article, profile line) is actually current and whether the question is genuinely answerable in one line. Don't just send more messages; a bad hook sent 5 times fails 5 times.
- **Replies coming but no calls booked:** the ask in message 2 may be too big too soon. Recheck that it's still perspective-only.
- **Calls happening but no referrals and no new names:** confirm the closing "who else should I talk to" question is actually being asked every single time. This is Su's named biggest miss.
- **Zero new contacts two weeks running:** that's the signal to block 60 to 70 minutes and refresh the target list (Dalton's LAMP exercise), not to send a rushed batch to catch up.

## Red flags and mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Asking for a job or a referral in message 1 | Perspective only in the first message. The referral ask waits for a real conversation (rule 3). |
| Sending the same message to everyone | Change the specific detail every time. If there isn't one yet, don't send it (rule 15). |
| Over-focusing on 2 to 3 companies ("artificial desperation," Dalton's term) | Keep 5 to 10 live conversations spread across multiple targets. |
| Giving nothing before asking | Lead with a comment or a real offer, one of the give-first assets above, before any ask (rule 10). |
| Skipping the closing "who else" question | Ask it at the end of every real conversation, with or without a specific role in mind. |
| Letting a thread go cold after one message | Run the day-3/day-7 follow-up, then stop. Don't ghost your own outreach. |
| Treating the call as a pitch instead of a set of questions | Use the 8-question script. Let them talk more than you do. |
| Naming anything confidential, unannounced, or off-limits from your current employer | Stay at the level of generality your employer's own public materials allow (rule 14). |
| Automating or batch-sending outreach | Every message goes out from your own hands, one at a time, personalized (rule 2). |
| Asking the same person for the same thing twice | Check the tracker before asking anything. If they already said no to a referral, ask for advice instead, not the same referral again (rule 12). |

## Before you hit send

1. Under 90 words, and under 300 characters if it's a connection note?
2. Checked this exact message for AI-sounding tells (buzzwords, em dashes, repeated openers, stock phrases) and fixed anything real?
3. No referral or job ask, if this is a first message?
4. Has one specific detail about them, one real point in common, one offer, and one easy either-or question?
5. Nothing confidential or unannounced, no internal figures, no comp history, no colleague names?
6. The hook (the post, article, or profile line cited) opened and verified live today, not from a stale search snippet?
7. Logged in the tracker before sending, with today's date, not after?
8. Read it out loud. Would you actually say this, in these words, to this person across a table?
